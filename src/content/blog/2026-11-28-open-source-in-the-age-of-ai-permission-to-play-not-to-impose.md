A year ago, contributing machine learning to a conference badge would have felt like an impossible task for most developers. Today, Martin Woodward built a pedometer onto his GitHub Universe badge using Copilot, trained a model in his browser, and deployed it in an afternoon. He walked around testing whether it could distinguish between walking, standing, and fidgeting. That's not a unicorn story anymore. That's just what happens when AI removes friction from the development process.

I think we're at an inflection point in how developers approach their craft. For decades, we've trained ourselves to plan meticulously, to think through every step before writing code. We built with everyone in mind. We perfected before shipping. But AI is fundamentally changing that calculus. When code generation can turn an idea into working implementation in minutes, the equation shifts. We can afford to tinker, to experiment, to build things just to see if they work.

This is exciting and liberating. It's also creating real friction in spaces that weren't designed for this pace of change.

## The Maintenance Crisis Nobody's Talking About Enough

According to the Octoverse report, 40% of pull requests were started by agents by September. That's a staggering jump from 17% in March. Most maintainers I've spoken with aren't celebrating this metric. They're exhausted.

Open source thrives on volunteer labor. A maintainer might spend years building something useful, and now they're drowning in AI-generated contributions. Some are good. Many aren't. The burden of review, testing, and decision-making has exploded, and the people carrying it weren't given a heads-up.

This isn't a failure of AI. It's a failure of community norm-setting. Martin said it plainly at the GitHub Universe keynote: "Open source is the freedom to inspect, to remix, and to share without asking for permission. But it's not the freedom to impose your will on others."

That hits different when you think about what "imposing your will" means now. It used to mean aggressive or opinionated contributions. Now it means flooding a repository with hundreds of PRs generated by a tool, without considering whether that maintainer actually wants or needs them.

## Why the Barrier to Entry Matters

I shouldn't downplay what's happening on the positive side. The lowering of barriers to entry is profound. Machine learning, embedded systems, full-stack development, game development on custom hardware - these were gatekept by complexity. Now they're accessible to anyone curious enough to try.

That's genuinely good for the industry. More experiments mean more innovation. More accessibility means more diverse perspectives solving problems. The GitHub Universe badges hacked by attendees prove this. People built games, apps, and tools just because they suddenly could.

But accessibility cuts both ways. Easier to build means easier to contribute carelessly. Easier to generate code means easier to generate noise.

## The Human Judgment Layer

This is where I think the actual work begins. AI can ship features faster than ever. It can't ship responsibility. We still need human judgment at every level: in writing (detecting the tells of AI prose), in imagery (those fonts never lie), in code (suspiciously well-commented blocks that suggest generation rather than understanding).

More importantly, we need humans in the feedback loop. That means inspecting what the agent produces, debugging it, testing it, making it your own. That means respecting the fact that open source maintainers aren't obligated to integrate every good idea into their project. If you want to experiment with [ai-development](https://mgks.dev/tags/ai-development/), fork the repository. Build your own version. That's the freedom open source actually provides.

Before you send something upstream, consider: Is this something the maintainer actually wants? Have I tested it thoroughly? Am I creating work for someone else, or genuinely solving a problem they've identified? These questions sound obvious until you're managing 50 AI-generated PRs in a week.

## Building for Yourself First

One of the most exciting shifts I'm seeing is that developers don't have to build for "everyone" anymore. Martin's pedometer wasn't designed for mass adoption. It didn't need to be enterprise-ready or handle edge cases across a thousand different use cases. It just needed to work on his badge, for his experiment.

That's liberating. It means more [open-source](https://mgks.dev/tags/open-source/) projects will exist just because someone thought "what if?" without needing a business model or billion-user scale. It means experimentation becomes the norm again.

But it also means we need clearer norms around when to share that experimentation and when to keep it private or forked. Not everything generated needs to be a pull request to a popular repository.

The real question isn't whether AI makes us better developers. It's whether we're thoughtful enough to use it responsibly while keeping open source healthy for the people who dedicate their time to it.
