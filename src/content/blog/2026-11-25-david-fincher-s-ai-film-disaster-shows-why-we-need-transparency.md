I've been watching the fallout from David Fincher's new film 'The Further Mis-Adventures of Cliff Booth' unfold online, and honestly? It's become a case study in why we desperately need clearer guidelines around AI in creative production.

For those not following the drama: early screenings of Fincher's Once Upon a Time in Hollywood sequel sparked immediate backlash. Directors and critics took to social media claiming the film was stuffed with what they're calling 'AI slop' - blatantly artificial-looking visuals that break the immersion. YouTuber Patrick Tomasso walked out feeling 'completely gutted,' describing the AI use as some of the most 'egregious' he's seen from a major studio. Even the trailers apparently feature weird shots of people sitting backward in horse-drawn carriages that scream 'generated imagery.'

Here's what gets me: Netflix acquired Ben Affleck's InterPositive startup for around $600 million specifically to develop AI tools that can tweak lighting, remove background elements, and replicate a director's signature style based on footage. That's a legitimately useful tool for post-production. But somewhere between 'useful tool' and 'final product,' something went very wrong.

## The Netflix Effect

Netflix's heavy involvement in this film's production combined with their massive investment in generative AI technology created a perfect storm of speculation. And honestly, that suspicion is justified. We're not being paranoid - we're being realistic about what happens when a company with AI infrastructure and financial pressure to innovate gets control of a major production.

Fincher himself didn't help matters. When asked about AI, he said he's 'not a Luddite' and questioned whether artists should stop iterating on their creative processes. His point about tools and evolution isn't entirely wrong. Tools matter. Artists have always used new technology. But there's a massive gap between using AI to tweak eye color and using it to generate entire sequences that look uncanny and inconsistent.

The problem isn't that Fincher considered using AI. The problem is that if he did use it extensively, we'd never know unless it looked this bad. And that's the real issue for us as creators and technologists.

## Why Transparency Actually Matters

I spend a lot of time thinking about [AI ethics](https://mgks.dev/tags/ai-ethics) in my work, and this situation perfectly illustrates why transparency isn't just some idealistic principle - it's practical. When audiences can't tell whether they're watching AI or actual filmmaking, trust erodes. When directors can deploy these tools silently, it sets a precedent that encourages worse behavior down the line.

Consider what this means for the broader industry. If Fincher - a director obsessed with control and detail - can ship a film full of visibly bad AI without immediate acknowledgment, what's stopping other productions from doing the same? What's stopping studios from replacing entire departments with cheaper AI generation?

Some critics suggested the bad visuals might just be bad CGI rather than AI-generated imagery. That's a charitable interpretation, but it misses the point. Whether it's AI or just poor execution, audiences deserve to know what they're watching. They deserve honesty about the creative process.

## What This Means for Developers

If you're building tools in this space - whether that's video generation, image synthesis, or anything in between - this is your warning. The tools are good enough now that they *work*, but they're not good enough that you can hide them. And that creates a weird incentive structure where the pressure is simultaneously to improve quality and to deploy before the technology is truly ready.

I think about the developers who built InterPositive's models with genuine intentions about helping directors and improving workflows. I believe they had good reasons to be proud of their work. But somewhere in the transition from startup to Netflix acquisition, the narrative shifted from 'tool to enhance human creativity' to 'replace human work with generated content.'

That matters because it affects how the next generation of AI tools gets built. Do we build toward collaboration and transparency, or do we build toward concealment and cost-cutting?

## The Uncomfortable Question

The Cliff Booth situation forces us to ask something we've been avoiding: if the AI had been good enough that nobody noticed, would it be okay?

I don't think so. And neither do you.
