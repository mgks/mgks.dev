A year ago, I would have told you that using Claude Code felt like a productivity tax. The model would get things wrong often enough that correcting it took longer than just writing the code myself. Today, November 24th 2026, I genuinely can't imagine writing software without an AI partner.

That shift didn't happen gradually. Looking back over the past twelve months, I can identify three distinct moments where the capabilities of frontier models genuinely changed what felt possible.

## The Reliability Threshold

The first inflection point was Claude Opus 4.5, released exactly one year ago today. This wasn't the most flashy release. Testers reported that the model handled ambiguity differently. It reasoned about tradeoffs without hand-holding. When pointed at a complex, multi-system bug, Opus 4.5 just figured out the fix.

But here's what mattered for me personally: it was the first time the default result from Claude Code was generally the right thing. For most of my daily coding problems, Opus would nail it on the first try. It was clearly faster than working through the problems myself.

OpenAI hit a similar threshold around the same time with GPT-5.1 and their Codex agent. By January 2026, we weren't experimenting with these tools anymore. We were using them for actual work.

The shift here wasn't incremental. When a tool crosses from "neat experiment" to "genuine accelerant," something fundamental changes in how you approach your craft. I stopped thinking of these as features and started thinking of them as collaborators.

## Proactive Problem Solving

The second inflection point arrived in June with Claude Fable 5. This one feels harder to articulate, but it was real.

Fable class models, as I've come to define them, are relentlessly proactive. I asked Fable to help fix a display glitch in Safari. Instead of asking clarifying questions, it spun up its own pyobjc-framework-Quartz screenshot mechanism and built a CORS-enabled localhost logging server to help me debug it.

That's not following instructions. That's understanding intent and autonomously building infrastructure to accomplish it.

A more recent example: I gave Opus 5.5 and then Fable 5.2 access to five WebAudio microphones in a room full of boxes. The goal was simple and somewhat silly: find the source of an infuriating mysterious beep. The models triangulated it to within about 40 centimeters. Give it a goal, give it the right tools, and watch it cook.

This capability matters enormously for developers because it means you can point an AI at messy, real-world problems and trust it to figure out the auxiliary tools and infrastructure needed to solve them. That's fundamentally different from earlier models that needed you to anticipate and provide every piece of the puzzle.

## Understanding Taste and Fun

The third inflection point is the strangest, and I'm still processing what it means. Around September, when Opus 5.5 landed, something shifted in how frontier models approached creative constraints.

I'd been working on a simple Command & Conquer-style RTS game. It looked correct. The mechanics functioned. But it was boring. I told Opus 5.5, "This game isn't fun enough yet. Make it a LOT more fun." and walked away.

12 hours later, Opus had playtested new features, rebalanced gameplay systems, and transformed it from dull to deeply entertaining. That was the first time I'd used a model that appeared to understand fun.

Since then I've seen this pattern repeat across multiple game projects. When you give Opus 5.5 or Fable 5.2 clear creative constraints and ask them to optimize for beauty and engagement, they produce results that actually hold your attention.

This matters because game design has historically been an almost uniquely human domain. We're not talking about procedurally generated content anymore. We're talking about AI systems that can reason about emergent gameplay, pacing, and player psychology. The indie games community has noticed this too. There are now real culture wars brewing about whether GTA VI might be the last pre-generative AI blockbuster.

## What Changes Now

I don't think I'll ever go back to writing most of my code by hand. More importantly, I think we're entering a phase where the [inflection points happen faster](https://mgks.dev/tags/ai-engineering/), and the capabilities compound in unexpected ways.

The question isn't whether these tools are useful anymore. They're obviously useful. The question is whether we're witnessing the emergence of genuine AI engineers, or just very sophisticated code completion. And maybe the distinction [doesn't matter as much](https://mgks.dev/tags/llms/) as we think.

If the pattern holds, there will be another inflection point before this year ends.
