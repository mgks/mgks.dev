A year ago, dabbling with machine learning felt daunting for most developers. The barrier to entry was steep, the learning curve brutal, and the motivation to push through it had to be strong. Today, that's changed dramatically.

At GitHub Universe, I watched Martin Woodward teach himself machine learning by building a pedometer onto a conference badge using Copilot. He collected accelerometer data, trained a model in his browser, and deployed it to test whether it could distinguish walking from standing from fidgeting. The whole thing felt almost casual, like tinkering with side projects used to feel as a kid building with Lego blocks.

That shift matters more than it might seem on the surface.

## The Experiment Economy

When code ships faster, we have permission to be curious again. AI tools have fundamentally changed what's possible without deep expertise or months of planning. We can build things just to see if they'll work, without needing to know every step upfront. The learning isn't happening before we code anymore; it's happening as we build.

According to the Octoverse report, 40% of pull requests were started by agents by September, jumping from 17% in March. That pace of change can feel unsettling when we've spent decades honing our craft the traditional way. But I think there's something exciting happening here: developers are experimenting more because the friction is gone.

The GitHub Universe badges this year had color screens, rechargeable batteries, accelerometer sensors, and touch controls. The hacking that happened around those badges was remarkable. People weren't building them because they had to; they were building because they could and because it was fun. That's the spirit we need to protect.

## The Maintenance Tax

But here's where things get complicated. With AI tools making it easier to contribute, maintainers are getting absolutely hammered with pull requests. Many of them are low-quality, poorly thought out, or misaligned with the project's actual needs. The maintenance burden has never been higher, and it's threatening to make open source less enjoyable for the people keeping it alive.

Open source is fundamentally about freedom: the freedom to inspect, to remix, and to share without asking permission. But as Martin pointed out in the keynote, it's not the freedom to impose your will on others.

There's a real distinction between exploring your own ideas in a fork and imposing those ideas on a mainline repository. When you fork something, you have room to tinker, experiment, and build exactly what you need. That's powerful. But before you send those experiments upstream as a pull request, you need to think about whether they actually solve a problem the maintainers care about.

The solution isn't to stop experimenting. It's to be more intentional about where that experimentation lands.

## What Maintainers Actually Need

If you're contributing to [open-source projects](https://mgks.dev/tags/open-source/), consider: Does this solve a problem the maintainer has explicitly mentioned? Is it aligned with the project's direction? Have you tested it thoroughly? Can you maintain it long-term?

These questions sound basic, but they're easy to skip when AI makes creating contributions so frictionless. It takes discipline to time-box your experiments and know when to stop polishing something before sharing it.

The shift toward [ai-development](https://mgks.dev/tags/ai-development/) tools doesn't eliminate the need for human judgment; it makes human judgment more important. We need to be able to inspect what AI produced, debug it, test it, and make it our own. We need to understand whether a contribution makes sense before we propose it, not just whether we can generate it.

## The Permission to Tinker

The exciting part of this moment is real. We can build things for our exact use cases without thinking about everyone else. A project could "just work on my machine" and be feature-complete. That's genuinely liberating compared to the complexity we've traditionally built into software.

But that freedom comes with responsibility. The more easily we can contribute, the more carefully we need to think about whether we should. The easier it is to send a pull request, the more important it becomes to ask whether that PR is actually a gift to the maintainer or a burden.

Give yourself permission to play and call it learning. Time-box it. Ship it. Share it in your own fork if it's just for fun. But before you impose it on someone else's work, make sure you're respecting the labor that goes into maintaining that project.

That's how we keep open source not just sustainable, but genuinely fun for everyone involved.
